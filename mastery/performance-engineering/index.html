Performance Engineering Mastery

Performance engineering is the discipline of making systems fast and predictable under real load — measuring the right things (percentiles, not means), modelling capacity (Little's Law), finding where the time actually goes (profiling, USE/RED), and defending the tail (hedging, load shedding, bulkheads). This page pushes past "add a cache" into the mechanisms, numbers and war-stories a Staff interviewer will drill you on.

Interview Explanation — 30 seconds

Say this: "Performance engineering is about making latency predictable at the tail, not just fast on average. I optimize for p99/p99.9 because the mean lies — one slow dependency in a fan-out of 100 calls means most requests wait on the slowest one. I size concurrency with Little's Law (L = λ × W), find bottlenecks with profiling and the USE/RED methods, and protect the tail with timeouts, hedged requests, load shedding and backpressure. The rule: measure first, model capacity, then optimize the proven bottleneck — never guess."

Interview Explanation — 2 minutes

There are three orthogonal quantities people confuse: latency (time per request), throughput (requests per second), and utilization (fraction of a resource busy). They trade against each other — as utilization climbs toward 100%, queueing theory says latency goes to infinity (the M/M/1 curve). Good systems run at 60-70% utilization to leave headroom for the tail.

I never report a mean. I report percentiles: p50 is the typical experience, p99/p99.9 is what your heaviest users and your fan-out paths feel. At Google's scale a service called 100 times in parallel sees its p99 become the median user experience — that's tail-at-scale (Dean & Barroso). So I attack the tail: bounded timeouts, hedged/tied requests, and killing coordinated omission in load tests.

For capacity I use Little's Law: concurrency in the system L = arrival rate λ × mean residence time W. That sizes thread pools, connection pools and concurrency limits. For diagnosis I use USE (Utilization, Saturation, Errors — per resource, Brendan Gregg) for infrastructure and RED (Rate, Errors, Duration — per service, Tom Wilkie) for request-driven services. For "where does the time go" I use sampling profilers and CPU flame graphs, plus off-CPU analysis and eBPF for the time spent waiting. In Node specifically the killers are event-loop blocking, an undersized libuv thread pool (UV_THREADPOOL_SIZE), GC pauses, and missing stream backpressure. On databases it's N+1 queries, missing/misordered indexes, and undersized/oversized connection pools.

Say this: "Latency, throughput and utilization are three different axes. Optimizing one without watching the others is how you ship a system that's fast in the benchmark and falls over in production."

Internal Working

How a disciplined performance investigation actually runs, step by step:

  1. Establish the SLO/SLI. Define the metric (e.g. p99 latency < 200ms for GET /checkout) and the measurement point. Decide client-side vs server-side — they differ by network + queueing time.
  2. Characterize load. Measure arrival rate λ (RPS) and the distribution (Poisson-ish? bursty?). Bursts break averages; you size for the peak plus headroom.
  3. Apply Little's Law. L = λW. If λ = 2000 RPS and W = 50ms, in-flight concurrency L = 100. That tells you how many worker slots / connections you need. If your pool is smaller than L, requests queue and W inflates — a feedback spiral.
  4. USE sweep on resources. For CPU, memory, disk, network, locks: check Utilization, Saturation (run-queue length, swap, pool wait), Errors. The saturated-or-erroring resource is the bottleneck.
  5. RED sweep on services. Rate, Errors, Duration histograms per endpoint. Find the endpoint whose Duration tail moved.
  6. Profile the hot path. Sampling profiler (perf, --prof, 0x, clinic flame) collects stacks at ~99Hz → aggregate into a flame graph. Width = time. Wide plateau = the bottleneck function.
  7. Off-CPU analysis. If the CPU flame graph is idle but latency is high, the time is spent waiting (locks, I/O, GC, event-loop backlog). Use off-CPU flame graphs / eBPF (offcputime) or, in Node, event-loop-lag metrics.
  8. Form one hypothesis, change one thing, re-measure. Compare percentile histograms before/after with warmup discarded (JIT, cache fill). Beware coordinated omission distorting the "after".
  9. Defend the tail. Add bounded timeouts, retries with jitter + budget, hedged requests, circuit breakers, bulkheads and load shedding so a slow dependency can't consume all concurrency.
  10. Set headroom + alerts. Cap steady-state utilization (~60-70%), alert on saturation and p99 SLO burn rate, not on averages.
Watch out: The mean is dragged around by outliers and hides bimodality (a cache hit path at 2ms + a miss path at 200ms averages to a number that describes neither request). Always look at the histogram/percentiles.

Why was this built?

Early web systems were sized by intuition and load-tested with tools that measured throughput and average latency. That worked until fan-out architectures (microservices, sharded storage, scatter-gather search) made the tail the product. When one page assembles 50-200 backend calls, the slowest of them defines the page — so a service that's "fast on average" but has a 1% slow tail makes half your pages slow. Jeff Dean & Luiz Barroso's 2013 "The Tail at Scale" formalized why: with 100 parallel calls each having a 1% chance of >1s, ~63% of requests hit at least one slow call.

Before: people watched average CPU and average response time, over-provisioned blindly, and reacted to incidents. There was no shared vocabulary for "the resource is saturated" vs "the service is slow," and load tests with fixed-thread clients silently under-reported latency (coordinated omission). Performance engineering brought queueing theory (Little's Law, M/M/1), percentile-based SLOs, systematic methods (USE/RED), and low-overhead production profiling (flame graphs, eBPF) so you could find the real bottleneck instead of guessing.

Say this: "These aren't mutually exclusive. Methods (USE/RED) tell you where, profiling tells you why, queueing theory tells you how much headroom. Auto-scaling is the fix only once you've proven the bottleneck is capacity."

Advantages

  • Predictability: percentile-based SLOs + headroom mean latency stays bounded under bursts instead of collapsing at the queueing cliff.
  • Cost efficiency: right-sizing pools/instances from Little's Law avoids both over-provisioning spend and under-provisioning outages.
  • Fast diagnosis: USE/RED + flame graphs turn "it's slow" into "index missing on orders.user_id" in minutes.
  • Resilience: tail defenses (hedging, load shedding, circuit breakers, bulkheads) keep the system serving during partial failure.
  • Low-overhead in prod: sampling profilers and eBPF run at <1-3% overhead, so you diagnose the real workload, not a lab replica.

Drawbacks

  • Observer effect & overhead: instrumentation profiling and verbose tracing can themselves add latency and skew results.
  • Expertise-heavy: reading flame graphs, off-CPU analysis, queueing math and coordinated omission is a rare skill; easy to mis-conclude.
  • Non-determinism: noisy neighbors, JIT warmup, GC, NUMA and CPU frequency scaling make results hard to reproduce.
  • Diminishing returns / premature optimization: chasing p99.99 can cost 10x for a use case that doesn't need it.
  • Tooling gaps: managed/serverless environments hide kernel access (no perf/eBPF), limiting deep analysis.

Trade-offs

  • Latency vs throughput: batching and larger buffers raise throughput but add latency; Nagle's algorithm, request coalescing, and Kafka linger.ms all embody this.
  • Latency vs durability: fsync-per-write / acks=all is safe but slow; async/buffered is fast but can lose data on crash.
  • Utilization vs tail latency: packing servers to 90% saves money but pushes you up the M/M/1 curve — the tail explodes. Headroom is insurance you pay for.
  • Consistency vs latency: strong/quorum reads cost round-trips; reading from a local replica is fast but stale (PACELC's "else Latency").
  • Hedging vs load: hedged requests cut the tail but add duplicate work; only worth it above a percentile threshold with a small extra-load budget (~5%).
  • Cache hit ratio vs freshness: longer TTL = higher hit ratio = lower latency, but staler data and thundering-herd risk on expiry.

When NOT to use it

Watch out: Don't invest in deep performance engineering when — (1) the system is pre-product-market-fit and load is tiny; correctness and shipping matter more; (2) you haven't measured yet — optimizing without a profile is guessing; (3) the bottleneck is human/process (deploys, on-call), not machine; (4) a trivial vertical scale-up is cheaper than an engineer-month of tuning; (5) the SLO is already comfortably met — chasing p99.99 for a batch job nobody watches is waste. Premature optimization is real; so is premature micro-optimization that hurts readability for a path that runs once a day.

Production Architecture

A realistic latency-sensitive topology with tail defenses at every layer. The client hits an edge/CDN, then an API gateway that enforces a concurrency limit and load shedding, fanning out to services that each own a bulkheaded connection pool and a circuit breaker to downstreams, with a cache in front of the database.

flowchart LR
  C["Client"] --> E["Edge CDN"]
  E --> G["API Gateway limit and shed"]
  G --> S1["Service A"]
  G --> S2["Service B"]
  S1 --> BK["Circuit breaker and bulkhead"]
  BK --> CA["Redis cache"]
  CA -->|miss| DB["Primary DB"]
  DB --> RR["Read replicas"]
  S2 --> Q["Async queue"]
  Q --> W["Worker pool"]

How it works: the gateway caps in-flight requests (from Little's Law: L = λW at the SLO) and sheds excess with 429/503 + Retry-After rather than letting the queue grow unbounded. Each service isolates downstream calls in bulkheads (separate pools) so a slow dependency can't starve unrelated traffic, guarded by a circuit breaker that trips to fail-fast when error/latency thresholds breach. Reads hit Redis first (target hit ratio >90-95%); misses go to the primary or a read replica. Writes and non-blocking work go through the queue to a worker pool with its own concurrency limit and backpressure. Every hop has a bounded timeout smaller than its caller's (timeout budget) so failures surface fast instead of piling up.

Production Problems

  • Retry storms: a downstream blips, everyone retries, the retries multiply load, the downstream stays down. Fix: retry budgets, exponential backoff + full jitter, circuit breakers.
  • Connection pool exhaustion: pool smaller than in-flight demand → requests queue for a connection, latency climbs, health checks time out, cascade. Classic under-Little's-Law sizing miss.
  • Event-loop blocking (Node): a synchronous JSON.parse of a 50MB payload, sync crypto, or a big regex freezes all requests on that process; p99 across every endpoint spikes together.
  • GC pauses: stop-the-world major GC adds 10s-100s of ms to whatever request is unlucky — pure tail latency, invisible in the mean.
  • Cache stampede / thundering herd: a hot key expires, thousands of requests miss simultaneously and hammer the DB. Fix: request coalescing / singleflight, jittered TTL, stale-while-revalidate.
  • N+1 queries: ORM lazy-loads a query per row in a loop; 1 + N round trips. Latency scales with result size.
  • Noisy neighbors: co-tenant saturates shared CPU/disk/network; your p99 moves with no code change.
  • Coordinated omission in load tests: a closed-loop client with fixed threads can't send requests while blocked on a slow one, so it under-counts exactly the slow requests — your report says p99 = 20ms when reality is 2s.

Debugging Guide

Concrete signals and commands, layered from system to app:

# System / USE method (Linux)
uptime                     # load average vs core count = run-queue saturation
vmstat 1                   # r column (runnable), si/so (swap = mem saturation)
mpstat -P ALL 1            # per-CPU util, %steal (noisy neighbor / stolen time)
iostat -xz 1               # %util, await, aqu-sz for disks
pidstat -t 1              # per-thread CPU
ss -s ; ss -tn state time-wait | wc -l   # socket / TIME_WAIT pressure

# CPU flame graph (sampling profiler, ~1% overhead)
perf record -F 99 -a -g -- sleep 30
perf script | stackcollapse-perf.pl | flamegraph.pl &gt; cpu.svg

# Off-CPU (where it WAITS) via eBPF
offcputime-bpfcc -df 30 &gt; off.stacks   # blocked-time stacks

# Node.js
node --prof app.js ; node --prof-process isolate-*.log   # V8 tick profiler
clinic doctor -- node app.js        # event-loop delay, GC, CPU classification
clinic flame -- node app.js         # 0x-style flame graph
node --inspect  # then Chrome DevTools: heap snapshot (compare 3 snapshots for leaks)
# event loop lag: perf_hooks.monitorEventLoopDelay() -&gt; histogram p99

# Database
EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS) SELECT ...;   # Postgres: real rows, buffer hits, seq scans
SHOW ENGINE INNODB STATUS;               # MySQL: locks, buffer pool
# slow query log:  long_query_time=0.1 ; pg: log_min_duration_statement=100
Say this: "First question I ask: is the CPU busy or idle? Busy → CPU flame graph. Idle but slow → off-CPU / waiting → locks, I/O, GC, or event-loop backlog. That fork decides the whole investigation."

Scaling

Two axes: vertical (bigger box — more cores/RAM/faster NVMe; limited by Amdahl's serial fraction and NUMA effects) and horizontal (more instances behind a balancer, sharding/partitioning stateful tiers). Stateless services scale horizontally almost linearly; the hard part is always the stateful tier and the coordination.

flowchart TD
  LB["Load balancer"] --> A1["App 1"]
  LB --> A2["App 2"]
  LB --> A3["App N"]
  A1 --> R["Router by shard key"]
  A2 --> R
  A3 --> R
  R --> S1["Shard 1 primary and replicas"]
  R --> S2["Shard 2 primary and replicas"]
  R --> S3["Shard 3 primary and replicas"]
  • Little's Law for autoscaling: target concurrency L = λ × W; scale instance count so per-instance concurrency stays below the pool limit with headroom. Scale on saturation (queue depth, concurrency), not just CPU.
  • Sharding: pick a shard key with even distribution and no hot keys; consistent hashing to minimize reshuffles; watch for the celebrity/hot-partition problem.
  • Read scaling: read replicas + cache absorb read load; writes still bottleneck on the primary → shard writes or use CQRS.
  • Limits: connection count on the DB (Postgres degrades past a few hundred connections → use PgBouncer), cross-shard queries/joins, and the coordination/consensus cost.
  • Headroom: keep ~30-40% spare so a lost instance/AZ doesn't push survivors over the queueing cliff.

Security

  • DoS is a performance problem: rate limiting, concurrency limits and load shedding are your first DoS defense; without them a flood pushes utilization to 100% and the queueing cliff does the attacker's work.
  • Algorithmic complexity attacks: ReDoS (catastrophic backtracking regex), hash-collision DoS, zip/JSON bombs — a single crafted input can pin a CPU or block the event loop. Bound input sizes, use linear-time regex engines (RE2), timeouts.
  • Encryption cost: TLS termination and at-rest encryption add CPU/latency; offload TLS to the LB / hardware (AES-NI), reuse sessions (0-RTT/session tickets) to avoid handshake cost.
  • AuthN/AuthZ in the hot path: validate JWTs locally (cache JWKS) instead of a network call per request; cache authz decisions with short TTL.
  • Network isolation: keep DB/cache in private subnets; mTLS between services; a compromised noisy service shouldn't reach your primary directly.
  • Observability leaks: don't log full payloads/PII in traces you added for perf; profiling artifacts (heap snapshots) contain live data — treat as sensitive.

Common Mistakes

  • Reporting the mean. Fix: percentiles + histograms; alert on p99 SLO burn.
  • Averaging percentiles across instances. You cannot average p99s — aggregate the underlying histograms (HdrHistogram/t-digest) then compute the percentile.
  • Coordinated omission in load tests. Fix: open-loop generators (wrk2, Gatling constant arrival rate) that record intended send time.
  • No warmup. JIT, connection pools, and caches need warmup; discard the first N seconds.
  • Undersized/oversized connection pool. Fix: size from Little's Law and a formula (see below), not a round number.
  • Blocking the Node event loop. Fix: offload CPU work to worker_threads / native, stream large payloads, never sync I/O in a request path.
  • Optimizing before profiling. Fix: flame graph first; optimize the widest frame.
  • Retries without budget/backoff/jitter. Fix: exponential backoff + full jitter + retry budget + circuit breaker.
  • Ignoring backpressure in streams/queues. Fix: respect writable.write() return value / pipe, bound queues, shed load.
  • Running at 90% utilization "to save money". Fix: headroom for the tail and failover.

Interview Follow-up Questions

  1. Define latency, throughput and utilization — how do they relate?
    1. Answer pointer: three axes; M/M/1 shows latency → ∞ as utilization → 1; Little's Law links them.
  2. Why is the mean latency misleading? What do you report instead?
    1. Answer pointer: outliers + bimodality; report p50/p95/p99/p99.9 histograms.
  3. What is tail-at-scale and why does p99 become the median at fan-out?
    1. Answer pointer: 100 parallel calls, each 1% slow → ~63% of requests hit ≥1 slow call.
  4. Derive the pool size for 2000 RPS at 50ms service time.
    1. Answer pointer: Little's Law L = λW = 2000 × 0.05 = 100 concurrent; add headroom.
  5. How would you find whether a service is CPU-bound or waiting?
    1. Answer pointer: CPU utilization + on-CPU flame graph vs off-CPU/eBPF; idle CPU + high latency = waiting.
  6. Explain the USE and RED methods and when each applies.
    1. Answer pointer: USE per-resource (util/sat/errors) for infra; RED per-service (rate/errors/duration) for requests.
  7. What is coordinated omission and how does it corrupt load tests?
    1. Answer pointer: closed-loop client can't send during a slow response → under-samples slow requests; fix with open-loop constant-rate generators.
  8. How do hedged requests reduce tail latency and what's the cost?
    1. Answer pointer: send a duplicate after p95 elapses, take first response; ~5% extra load budget; cancel the loser.
  9. In Node, how do you detect and fix a blocked event loop?
    1. Answer pointer: monitorEventLoopDelay histogram; move CPU work to worker_threads, stream large I/O.
  10. What is UV_THREADPOOL_SIZE and when do you change it?
    1. Answer pointer: libuv pool (default 4) for fs/crypto/dns; raise for parallel blocking ops, bounded by cores.
  11. How do GC pauses show up in latency and how do you mitigate?
    1. Answer pointer: pure tail spikes; reduce allocations, tune heap size, use object pools, watch major-GC frequency.
  12. Diagnose an N+1 query problem end to end.
    1. Answer pointer: slow log/trace shows many identical queries; EXPLAIN; fix with join/batch/eager load or DataLoader.
  13. How do you size a DB connection pool? Why not "just make it huge"?
    1. Answer pointer: Little's Law + a formula; too many connections thrash the DB (context switch, lock contention) — use a proxy like PgBouncer.
  14. What happens as utilization approaches 100%? Show the math.
    1. Answer pointer: M/M/1 W = 1/(µ−λ); wait time blows up; hence 60-70% target.
  15. How do circuit breakers, bulkheads and load shedding differ?
    1. Answer pointer: breaker = fail fast on unhealthy dep; bulkhead = isolate resource pools; shedding = reject excess to protect the rest.
  16. A downstream slows down and everything cascades. Walk me through the failure and the fixes.
    1. Answer pointer: in-flight grows (Little's Law), pool exhausts, retries amplify; fix with timeouts < caller, budgets+jitter, breaker, shed.
  17. How do you prevent a cache stampede on a hot key?
    1. Answer pointer: singleflight/request coalescing, jittered TTL, stale-while-revalidate, lock+refresh.
  18. Your p99 doubled with no deploy. What are your first three checks?
    1. Answer pointer: noisy neighbor/%steal, GC/heap growth, downstream latency + cache hit-ratio drop.
  19. How would you set an SLO and alert on it without pager fatigue?
    1. Answer pointer: multi-window multi-burn-rate error-budget alerts, not static thresholds.
  20. When is optimizing p99.9 the wrong priority?
    1. Answer pointer: when SLO already met, load tiny, or cost/complexity outweighs user impact; Amdahl says fix the real bottleneck.

Common Cross Questions

  • After latency/percentiles → they ask about observability: distributed tracing (spans, sampling), metrics vs logs vs traces, histogram aggregation (t-digest/HdrHistogram). Angle: you can't average percentiles; aggregate underlying distributions.
  • After Little's Law → queueing theory: M/M/1, M/M/c, Erlang-C, why bursts break averages. Angle: utilization vs wait-time curve, headroom.
  • After tail latency → resilience patterns: retries/backoff/jitter, circuit breakers, bulkheads, load shedding, backpressure. Angle: bound everything, isolate blast radius.
  • After Node event loop → concurrency models: event loop vs thread-per-request vs goroutines, async/await, worker_threads, libuv. Angle: pick model to match CPU vs I/O bound.
  • After DB performance → data modelling & indexing: B-tree vs hash vs covering indexes, composite index column order, query planner, buffer pool. Angle: index for the WHERE+ORDER BY, watch write amplification.
  • After caching → cache design: eviction (LRU/LFU/ARC), write-through vs write-back, invalidation, consistency, hot keys. Angle: hit ratio vs freshness trade-off.
  • After scaling → distributed systems: sharding, consistent hashing, CAP/PACELC, replication lag, hotspots. Angle: shard key choice and cross-shard cost.
  • After profiling → CPU internals: cache lines, false sharing, branch prediction, NUMA, SIMD. Angle: memory locality dominates modern perf.

Production Scenarios

Scenario 1 — p99 latency spike with flat p50 (GC / tail)

Symptomsp50 unchanged at 8ms, p99 jumped from 40ms to 900ms; CPU and RPS flat; sporadic timeouts.
InvestigationRED histogram shows a growing high-latency bucket; correlate with GC logs / clinic doctor; heap growing between majors.
Root CauseA memory leak (unbounded cache map) forced frequent long stop-the-world major GCs that froze random requests.
SolutionBound the cache (LRU with max size), fix retained references (heap snapshot diff), tune heap; p99 back to 45ms.
PreventionAlert on GC pause p99 and heap-after-GC trend; enforce bounded caches; heap-snapshot canary in CI.
Interview Answer"Flat mean, spiking tail screamed GC. Heap-after-major was climbing — a leak. Snapshot diff pointed at an unbounded map; bounding it killed the pauses. Lesson: the mean hides tail-only pathologies like GC."

Scenario 2 — Cascading failure from a slow dependency

SymptomsOne downstream got slow; within 90s the whole service returned 503s, threads/connections all busy.
InvestigationPool in-use = 100%, wait time high; traces show all requests parked on the slow dependency; retries doubling downstream load.
Root CauseNo timeout/bulkhead: slow dep held connections (Little's Law: W↑ → L↑ → pool exhausted), and blind retries amplified the load (retry storm).
SolutionAdd per-dependency timeout (< caller budget), bulkhead pool, circuit breaker, backoff+jitter+retry budget; shed excess with 503+Retry-After.
PreventionStandardize timeouts/breakers in the RPC library; chaos-test dependency latency; alert on pool saturation.
Interview Answer"A single slow dep consumed all concurrency because we had no timeout or isolation — Little's Law made in-flight explode and retries amplified it. Bulkheads + breakers + budgeted retries turned a cascade into a contained, fail-fast degradation."

Scenario 3 — Load test said 20ms, production p99 was 2s

SymptomsBenchmark reported great p99; real users saw multi-second stalls under identical RPS.
InvestigationLoad tool was closed-loop with fixed threads; server had periodic 1-2s stalls (GC/lock) the client couldn't sample because it was blocked.
Root CauseCoordinated omission: the client stopped issuing requests during slow responses, so it under-counted exactly the slow ones.

Scenario 4 — Database slow after a "harmless" release

SymptomsEndpoint latency 5x; DB CPU near 100%; RPS unchanged; started right after a deploy.
InvestigationSlow query log lit up; EXPLAIN ANALYZE showed a sequential scan where an index scan used to run; buffer cache hit ratio dropped.
Root CauseA new WHERE clause / column reorder no longer matched the composite index prefix, so the planner fell back to full scans (and an N+1 in the new code path).
SolutionAdd/adjust composite index to match predicate + sort order; batch the N+1 with a single IN query; latency restored.
PreventionEXPLAIN diff in CI, slow-query alerting, index-usage review on schema/query changes.
Interview Answer"Latency 5x with flat traffic and pegged DB CPU means query plan regression. EXPLAIN showed a seq scan — the new query didn't match the index prefix. Fixing the composite index and collapsing an N+1 into one IN query restored it."

🎯 Deep-dive Q&A — model answers

Why does p99 of a dependency become the median experience under fan-out, and what's the exact math?

If a request requires N parallel sub-calls and each has independent probability p of exceeding threshold T, the probability the overall request is fast (all under T) is (1−p)^N. With p=0.01 and N=100, P(all fast)=0.99^100 ≈ 0.366, so ~63.4% of requests hit at least one slow call — the 99th-percentile behavior of one backend becomes worse-than-median at the request level. That's the tail-at-scale result. Fixes: reduce N (coarser calls), reduce p (fix the backend tail), or hide it — hedged/tied requests, request cancellation, and returning partial results. Crucially, adding replicas doesn't help if the slowness is correlated (shared GC pause, shared lock, shared queue).

Walk me through sizing a connection pool with a real formula, not a guess.

Start from Little's Law: in-flight DB work L = λ × W, where λ is DB-query RPS and W is mean query time. That's the minimum concurrency. But the DB has its own optimum: too many connections cause context-switch thrash and lock contention. HikariCP's guidance: connections ≈ ((core_count × 2) + effective_spindle_count) as a starting point for the DB server, and it's usually a small number (e.g. 10-20 per node), fronted by a pool. So you size the app pool from Little's Law but cap total DB connections near the DB's sweet spot, using a proxy (PgBouncer in transaction mode) to multiplex many app connections onto few DB connections. Signs you're wrong: pool wait time > 0 (too small) or DB CPU high on context switching / lock waits with low useful throughput (too big).

How exactly do hedged requests work and how do you bound their cost?

Send the request to one replica; if no response by a deadline (e.g. the observed p95), send a second (hedged) request to another replica and take whichever returns first, cancelling the loser. Because you only hedge the slow tail (say 5% of requests), extra load is ~5%, but you cut p99/p99.9 dramatically because it's rare for two independent replicas to both be slow. "Tied requests" (Google) go further: enqueue on two servers, each tells the other it started so the second dequeues — near-zero duplicate work. Bound cost with a global hedge budget (cap % of hedged traffic) so a broad slowdown doesn't double total load and worsen the very saturation you're fighting.

Explain the M/M/1 latency curve and why 70% utilization is a common target.

For an M/M/1 queue (Poisson arrivals λ, exponential service µ), utilization ρ=λ/µ and mean time in system W = 1/(µ−λ) = (1/µ)/(1−ρ). So wait time scales with 1/(1−ρ): at ρ=0.5, W is 2× service time; at ρ=0.9, 10×; at ρ=0.99, 100×. The curve is a hockey stick — near saturation, tiny load increases cause huge latency jumps, and bursts (real traffic isn't perfectly smooth) push you over. Running at ~60-70% keeps you on the flat part with headroom for bursts and instance/AZ loss. This is why "our CPU is only 70%, why is it slow?" is a wrong question — 70% is where a bursty tail already suffers.

A CPU flame graph is mostly idle but latency is high. What now?

The time is off-CPU — spent waiting, not computing. Do off-CPU analysis: eBPF offcputime aggregates stacks by blocked time, showing whether threads block on locks (futex), disk/network I/O, or condition variables. In Node the analogue is event-loop delay (monitorEventLoopDelay) plus async stack traces — high lag with idle CPU means the loop is starved or work is queued behind a blocking op in the libuv pool. The diagnostic fork is: on-CPU flame graph answers "what burns CPU," off-CPU answers "what do we wait for." Most latency problems in I/O-heavy services are off-CPU, which is exactly why CPU-only profiling misleads people.

Why can't you average p99s across instances, and what do you do instead?

Percentiles are non-linear order statistics; the mean of per-instance p99s is not the fleet p99 (imagine one instance at 1000ms p99 with 1 request and another at 10ms p99 with a million requests — the average is meaningless). You must merge the underlying distributions. Practically, each instance exports a mergeable sketch — HdrHistogram (fixed relative-error bucketed histogram) or a t-digest (accurate at the extremes) — and the backend sums the buckets, then reads the percentile from the merged histogram. Prometheus histogram_quantile does this over bucket counts. Also beware bucket boundaries: too-coarse buckets make p99.9 a lie.

How do you kill a cache stampede on a single hot key?

When a hot key expires, thousands of concurrent misses hit the DB simultaneously (thundering herd). Fixes, layered: (1) singleflight / request coalescing — only one caller recomputes, the rest await its result; (2) jittered TTL so keys don't expire in lockstep; (3) stale-while-revalidate — serve the stale value and refresh asynchronously so no request blocks; (4) probabilistic early expiration (XFetch) — recompute slightly before expiry with probability rising as TTL nears; (5) a short-lived lock so only one process rebuilds. For truly hot keys also consider local (in-process) caching in front of Redis to cut network trips and add a second coalescing layer.

What is coordinated omission and how do open-loop generators fix it?

A closed-loop load client has fixed threads that send request, wait for response, send next. When the server stalls for 1s, that thread simply doesn't issue the ~50 requests it would have during that second — so the slow period is under-sampled and those omitted requests (which would have measured ~1s waits due to queueing) never appear. Result: the tool reports a beautiful p99 while users see seconds. The fix is an open-loop model with a fixed arrival rate: the generator schedules request send-times independently of responses and, when a response is late, backfills the intended latency (send-time to receive-time) for requests that "should" have gone out. Tools: wrk2, Gatling (constant arrival rate), JMeter with precise-throughput timer. Always warm up and discard the ramp.

Describe how you'd tune a Node.js service that's slow under load.

Step 1: is the event loop blocked? monitorEventLoopDelay histogram — p99 >100ms means synchronous work in the request path (JSON.parse of huge bodies, sync crypto, regex, sync fs). Move it to worker_threads or native, and stream large payloads with proper backpressure (respect writable.write()'s false return / use pipeline). Step 2: is the libuv thread pool saturated? fs/crypto/dns.lookup/zlib run on a pool of default size 4 — if you do many concurrent blocking ops, raise UV_THREADPOOL_SIZE (up to ~cores) or reduce blocking calls. Step 3: GC — clinic doctor / --prof; reduce allocations, bound caches, tune --max-old-space-size. Step 4: flame graph (clinic flame / 0x) for hot JS. Step 5: heap snapshots (3-snapshot diff in DevTools) for leaks — look at retained size and detached DOM/closures. Step 6: scale horizontally with cluster/PM2 since one process is single-threaded for JS.

Distinguish circuit breaker, bulkhead, load shedding, and backpressure — when do you reach for each?

Circuit breaker: wraps a call to a dependency; after error/latency thresholds it "opens" and fails fast (skipping the call) for a cooldown, then half-opens to probe recovery — stops you wasting resources and hammering a sick downstream. Bulkhead: partitions resources (separate connection/thread pools per dependency or tenant) so one saturated path can't consume all capacity — blast-radius isolation. Load shedding: when you're over capacity, proactively reject/deprioritize excess requests (429/503, drop low-priority) to keep the rest within SLO — protects the whole system's health. Backpressure: a flow-control signal upstream to slow the producer to the consumer's rate (TCP windows, reactive streams, bounded queues) — prevents unbounded buffering and OOM. You typically use all four together: bulkheads isolate, breakers fail fast, backpressure regulates flow, shedding is the last resort at the edge.

How do you design an alert that catches SLO violations without paging on every blip?

Use multi-window, multi-burn-rate error-budget alerts (Google SRE). Define the SLO (e.g. 99.9% of requests < 200ms over 28 days → 0.1% error budget). Alert when you're burning budget fast enough to matter: e.g. page if the last 1h AND last 5m both show a 14.4× burn rate (would exhaust the month's budget in ~2 days), warn on a slower 6× burn over 6h/30m windows. The short window makes it responsive; the long window confirms it's sustained, killing single-spike false pages. This beats a static "p99 > 200ms for 5m" threshold, which pages on noise and ignores the budget you actually have.

What's Amdahl's Law and how does it cap your scaling?

If fraction s of the work is inherently serial and (1−s) parallelizable, maximum speedup with N processors is 1/(s + (1−s)/N), approaching 1/s as N→∞. So if 5% is serial, you can never go faster than 20×, no matter how many cores. Implication: adding hardware has diminishing returns; find and shrink the serial section (locks, single coordinator, global counter, a single hot partition). Gustafson's Law reframes it for growing problem sizes (weak scaling), but for a fixed request Amdahl rules. In distributed systems the "serial fraction" is often coordination/consensus or a hot shard — which is why shard-key design and lock reduction matter more than raw node count.

Buffer cache hit ratio is 99.5% but the DB is still slow. What's going on?

A high hit ratio only means pages are in RAM, not that queries are efficient. You can be CPU-bound scanning millions of in-memory rows (missing index → seq scan of cached pages), or blocked on locks, or doing huge sorts/hashes that spill. EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS) shows shared hit vs read (confirming cache) but also rows scanned vs returned — a 1000:1 ratio screams missing index or bad selectivity. Check lock waits (pg_stat_activity wait_event), sort/hash spills to temp (work_mem too small), and plan regressions. So don't chase hit ratio as a goal; it's necessary-not-sufficient. The real questions: how many rows are we touching per row returned, and are we waiting on locks or spilling?

How do you benchmark correctly given JIT, warmup, and noisy neighbors?

(1) Warm up: run enough iterations to trigger JIT compilation, fill connection pools and caches, then discard warmup samples. (2) Steady state: measure over a long enough window that GC and periodic effects are represented. (3) Isolate the environment: dedicated instance, pin CPU frequency (disable turbo/governor variance), check %steal, avoid burstable instances. (4) Use open-loop constant-arrival-rate to avoid coordinated omission. (5) Report the full distribution (p50/p99/p99.9 + max), not mean/throughput alone, and include variance across multiple runs. (6) Control confounders: same data, same network path, one variable per experiment. (7) For microbenchmarks use a harness (JMH/benchmark.js) that handles dead-code elimination and statistical rigor. The cardinal sin is a single hot loop on an idle laptop reported as a mean — it predicts nothing about production.

Your service's p99 is fine but users complain it's slow. Reconcile that.

Server-side p99 excludes what the user actually experiences: DNS, TLS handshake, network RTT (especially cross-region/mobile), client-side rendering/JS, and — critically — the queueing before your measurement point (if you time from "handler start," you miss time spent waiting in the accept queue / load balancer). Also client p99 aggregates the whole page's fan-out, so a slow asset or third-party beacon dominates. Fixes: measure at the edge/client (RUM — real user monitoring), include connection setup, watch the accept queue and LB latency, and remember tail-at-scale on the page's resource fan-out. The gap between server p99 and user-perceived latency is usually network + queueing + client, and it's where real UX lives.

How do timeouts, retries, and budgets interact in a call chain, and how do you avoid amplification?

Each hop's timeout must be strictly smaller than its caller's remaining budget (a deadline propagated down the chain, e.g. gRPC deadlines), otherwise the caller times out while the callee keeps working — wasted effort and orphaned load. Retries multiply load: a naive retry-3-times at every layer turns 1 request into up to 3×3×3=27 downstream calls during an incident (retry amplification / storm). Defenses: (1) propagate a deadline, not a per-hop timeout; (2) retry only at one layer, ideally the outermost with visibility; (3) exponential backoff with full jitter to de-correlate; (4) a retry budget (e.g. retries ≤ 10% of requests) so retries can't dominate; (5) circuit breakers to stop retrying a dead dependency; (6) mark requests idempotent so retries are safe. The goal: retries help a transient blip but can't turn a brownout into a full outage.
